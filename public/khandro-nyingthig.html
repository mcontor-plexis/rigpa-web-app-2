Khandro Nyingthig
The Heart Essence of the Dakinis

མཁའ་འགྲོ་སྙིང་གི་ཐིག་ལེ།  ·  mkha' 'gro snying thig

A terma cycle revealed across centuries — sealed by Padmasambhava in a dying princess, concealed by Yeshe Tsogyal, unearthed by the tertön Pema Ledreltsel, and crowned by Longchenpa’s luminous Yangtig commentaries.

A Deep Research Presentation
Prologue

A Cycle Entrusted to the Dakinis

Of the great Nyingma Dzogchen cycles, the Khandro Nyingthig — the Heart Essence of the Dakinis — holds a singular place. It is the feminine counterpart to Vimalamitra’s Vima Nyingthig: where the latter preserves the Indian scholar-adept’s transmission, the Khandro Nyingthig preserves the line given by Padmasambhava himself, sealed within the heart of a Tibetan princess and guarded for five centuries by a wrathful dakini protector before being set loose in the world.1

Together, these two “mothers” — with Longchenpa’s three “child” commentaries — form the Nyingtik Yabzhi, the Fourfold Heart Essence: a thirteen-volume collection that stands, by near-universal agreement, among the most important bodies of Dzogchen literature ever compiled in Tibet.2

“Guru Rinpoche’s Khandro Nyingtik is called the ‘mother text’, as is Vimalamitra’s Vima Nyingtik, and so these two are known as the two ‘mothers’ of the entire Fourfold Heart Essence.” — Rigpa Wiki, Khandro Nyingtik entry

Philology

What “Khandro Nyingthig” Means

The Tibetan mkha’ ’gro snying thig reads, almost word for word, “sky-goer heart-drop.” Khandro (mkha’ ’gro) renders the Sanskrit Ḍākinī — “she who moves in the sky” — the luminous feminine principle of awakened energy, simultaneously a goddess, a protector, a meditative experience, and a class of realized women.3 Nyingthig (snying thig) is usually translated “heart essence” or “heart drop,” denoting the innermost, most distilled class of Dzogchen pith instructions.4

The cycle is called Khandro Nyingthig for three interwoven reasons. First, Padmasambhava originally entrusted it to dakinis — to Yeshe Tsogyal and to Princess Pema Sal, who were themselves regarded as dakini emanations. Second, the guardian of the teaching was the protectress Shaza Khamoche, a wrathful dakini who kept the terma sealed until the prophesied hour. Third, when the cycle finally surfaced in the fourteenth century, it surfaced through visions, prophecies, and interventions of dakini figures throughout.1

Eighth Century

The Syllable Nri on the Heart of a Dying Princess

The origin story is among the most poignant in Tibetan literature. During Padmasambhava’s years at the imperial court of King Trisong Detsen (c. 742–796), the king’s young daughter Princess Pema Sal (Lhacham Pemasal) fell suddenly and gravely ill at a tantric feast in the mountains above Samye. Before the adepts present could intervene, she died — eight years old.5

Padmasambhava did not bring her back to life. He did something stranger, and more precise. He drew the red syllable NRI — the seed-syllable of human beings — upon her heart, spoke her name to summon her consciousness, and for a brief window restored her to awareness. In that window he conferred upon her the full empowerment and mind-mandate transmission of the Khandro Nyingthig, sealing the teaching within her awareness-stream. Then he let her pass.56

Padmasambhava prophesied that she would one day return, in a different body, and would open the teaching to the world. He then dictated the full cycle to Yeshe Tsogyal, who sealed it as terma — hidden treasure — at the rock of Daklha Tramo Drak in the Dakpo region of southern Tibet. The wrathful protectress Shaza Khamoche was entrusted with its guardianship.17

“Guru Rinpoche wrote the syllable nri on Lhacham Pemasal’s heart, and said her name, thus summoning her consciousness back, and she returned to life. He gave the empowerment, transmissions, and teachings, and predicted that the Khandro Nyingthig would be revealed in the world.” — Tibetan Buddhist Encyclopedia
14th Century

Pema Ledreltsel — The Princess Returns

Five centuries after her death, Pema Sal took rebirth as Pema Ledreltsel (Padma las ’brel rtsal, “Lotus Endowed with Karmic Links”), born in the Dakpo region of southern Tibet. The dates most commonly given are 1291–1315, though some sources give slight variants.8

At fifteen, Pema Ledreltsel received the prophecy announcing him as the destined revealer of the Khandro Nyingthig. At twenty-two he withdrew the terma from its place of concealment at Daklha Tramo Drak, exactly the rock Yeshe Tsogyal had sealed half a millennium earlier. The revelation is traditionally dated to 1313 (though sources vary slightly).9

Pema Ledreltsel’s life was, by the standards of Tibetan tertön biographies, shockingly brief — he died young, around twenty-four, leaving the cycle to his chief disciple Gyalse Lekden (also called Shuk Gyalse Lekpa) to transmit forward. Before dying, he is said to have declared that he himself would take rebirth once more, and this next rebirth would complete what his short life could only begin.10

The Oral Transmission from Special Individuals

The line that runs from Padmasambhava — Yeshe Tsogyal — Princess Pema Sal — Pema Ledreltsel — Gyalse Lekden — Longchenpa is classified in the tradition as the Oral Transmission from Special Individuals (gang zag snyan brgyud) — distinguished from the longer transmission streams that run through dozens of hands. It is short, direct, and regarded as carrying a particular potency precisely because of its compression.1

1308–1364

Longchenpa and the Khandro Yangtig

Pema Ledreltsel’s prophesied next rebirth was Longchen Rabjam — Longchenpa — born in 1308 in central Tibet, widely held to be the greatest systematizer and scholar-yogin in the entire Nyingma tradition.11 He received the full Khandro Nyingthig transmission from Pema Ledreltsel’s chief disciple Gyalse Lekden, thereby preserving the unbroken lineage of Guru Rinpoche’s own line.12

The Empowerment at Samye Chimphu

At age thirty-three — around 1341 — Longchenpa bestowed the complete Khandro Nyingthig cycle to eight fortunate disciples, men and women together, at the sacred cave complex of Samye Chimphu above the Samye monastery. According to the traditional accounts, during the empowerment the guardian goddess Ekajati entered the body of a yogini present and gave prophecies; a rain of flowers fell; multicolored rainbow light filled the sky; and Longchenpa himself experienced visions of Padmasambhava and Yeshe Tsogyal. He had been called to this teaching, a few months earlier, by a dark-skinned woman who appeared to him holding a Khandro Nyingthig text, handed it to him, and vanished — understood in the tradition as an emanation of the protectress Sokdrupma.13

The Khandro Yangtig — Mind Treasure

Out of these visions, and during years of retreat at his hermitage Gangri Thökar south of Lhasa, Longchenpa composed his own companion cycle to the root terma: the Khandro Yangtig (mkha’ ’gro yang tig, “Innermost Essence of the Dakinis”). It is not an ordinary commentary. Its individual texts declare, in their colophons, whether they are compositions or mind treasures (dgongs gter) — meaning Longchenpa received portions of the cycle directly, through revelation, rather than through intellectual composition alone.14

The Khandro Yangtig runs to three volumes and contains the essential practice instructions for the root cycle. Its three most central treatises are the Precious Treasury of Meaningful Words, the Precious Golden Garland of Instructions, and the Cloud Oceans of the Profound Meaning — alongside the Trilogy of Space and the Secret Treasury of the Dakinis, among others.15

“Longchenpa is said to have received and transcribed the Khandro Yangtig following his reception of the Khandro Nyingtik, after experiencing a state of meditative clarity and experiencing visions of Padmasambhava and Yeshe Tsogyel.” — Buddha Visions, on the Khandro Yangtig
Architecture

The Fourfold Heart Essence — Nyingtik Yabzhi

Longchenpa’s achievement was not merely to preserve one stream. He organized the entire body of the oldest Dzogchen “Heart-Essence” literature into a single, coherent architecture. The resulting collection, the Nyingtik Yabzhi (snying thig ya bzhi, “Fourfold Heart Essence”), condenses the two great lineages of Dzogchen practice in Tibet — that of Vimalamitra and that of Padmasambhava — into one unified corpus.2

Its internal structure is traditionally described through the metaphor of mothers and children:

Vima Nyingthig

The “mother” text transmitted from Vimalamitra — the root Indian Dzogchen Menngagde teachings. Four volumes.

Lama Yangtig

Longchenpa’s “child” commentary on the Vima Nyingthig: the Wish-Fulfilling Jewel. Two volumes.

Khandro Nyingthig

The “mother” text transmitted from Padmasambhava through Princess Pema Sal and Pema Ledreltsel. Two volumes.

Khandro Yangtig

Longchenpa’s “child” — partly composed, partly mind treasure — commentary on the Khandro Nyingthig. Three volumes.

Zabmo Yangtig

“The Profound Innermost Essence” — Longchenpa’s synthesis drawing from both mother cycles together. Two volumes.

The thirteen-volume edition of the Nyingtik Yabzhi contains more than 380 individual texts, ranging across history, biography, tantra, liturgy, pith instruction, empowerment ritual, and poetic song. It is, by common assent, the single most comprehensive corpus of Dzogchen’s innermost menngagde transmission preserved in any language.1622

“The Vima Nyingtik and Khandro Nyingtik are the ‘mother’ texts, and the Lama Yangtik and Khandro Yangtik are the ‘child’ texts — hence the common name Four Mother-and-Child Sections of the Nyingtik.” — Dezhung Rinpoche, Survey of the Nyingtik Yabzhi
The Path

What One Actually Does

Beyond its historical and textual grandeur, the Khandro Nyingthig is, first and last, a contemplative practice. Its structure follows the classical Dzogchen menngagde progression — preliminaries, trekchö, tögal — carried by the distinctive imagery and empowerments of the dakini line.

The Six Root Tantras of Liberation-by-Wearing

Two major text-clusters form the root of the Khandro Nyingthig cycle. The first is the Six Tantras of Liberation through Wearing (btags grol rgyud drug) — root tantras so named because to wear a written copy sealed in a protection-amulet is itself held to sow seeds of liberation:17

I · Essence Tantra

The Liberation by Wearing Essence Tantra — the heart of the cluster.

II · Knowing One

The Knowing One Thing All Is Liberated tantra.

III · Golden Syllable

The Single Golden Syllable of the Quintessence.

IV · Single Child

The Seed of the Single Child tantra.

V · Self-Arisen

The Self-Arisen Awareness tantra.

VI · Fruition

The Complete Great Perfection of the Fruition tantra.

The Three Testaments of the Buddha

The second cluster is the Three Testaments (sangs rgyas kyi ’das rjes gsum pa) — three short texts of Dzogchen instruction attributed to Vajradhara speaking to Garab Dorje, the first human Dzogchen lineage holder. The first is an extremely succinct summary of preliminaries, trekchö, and tögal together; the second focuses on preliminaries of body, speech, and mind; the third on the crucial points of body, speech, and mind for the practice of tögal.17

Trekchö and Tögal — Severing and Direct Crossing

The fourth, innermost empowerment of the Khandro Nyingthig cycle — the precious word empowerment — is said to be equivalent to, and to introduce, the complete path of Dzogchen: both trekchö (“cutting through” to primordial purity) and tögal (“direct crossing” into spontaneously present luminous form). The cycle is therefore not a system of symbols alone but an entire Dzogchen path, delivered in dakini voice.18

The Fourfold Dakini

The Khandro Nyingthig makes extensive use of a fourfold yidam structure in which the dakini appears in progressively inner and more esoteric forms:

Outer

Yeshe Tsogyal — the historical dakini of Tibet.

Inner

Kurukullā — the dakini of magnetizing activity.

Secret

Siṃhamukha (Senge Dongma) — the lion-faced wisdom dakini.

Innermost

Tröma Nakmo — the wrathful Black Wrathful Mother.

This fourfold progression, found elaborated in the closely related cycle Khandro Tuktik, reappears across later revelations in the Nyingthig stream — including in Dudjom Lingpa’s Tröma — demonstrating the Khandro Nyingthig’s continuing structural influence upon subsequent Dzogchen termas.19

Into the Present

The Living Lineage

The Khandro Nyingthig has never been an exclusive possession of a single monastery or tulku line. Because Longchenpa wove it into the Nyingtik Yabzhi, and because the Yabzhi is part of the foundational curriculum of the Nyingma menngagde transmission, the Khandro Nyingthig has been held and passed forward by virtually every major Nyingma master who ever held the full Dzogchen transmission.

14th Century
Longchen Rabjam (1308–1364)
Receives the cycle from Gyalse Lekden; composes the Khandro Yangtig at Gangri Thökar; assembles the Nyingtik Yabzhi; transmits at Samye Chimphu.
18th Century
Jigme Lingpa (1730–1798)
The seer of Longchen Nyingthig, who receives the entire Nyingtik Yabzhi transmission and recasts the Khandro/Vima material into a distilled gongter cycle that becomes the most practiced Dzogchen system of modern times.
19th Century
Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo (1820–1892)
Central figure of the Rimé movement; preserves and re-transmits the complete Nyingtik Yabzhi, safeguarding the Khandro Nyingthig against the textual attrition of his era.
20th Century
Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche (1910–1991)
After the passing of Jamyang Khyentse Chökyi Lodrö, he becomes the principal holder of the Khyentse lineage and one of the most important modern transmitters of the Khandro Nyingthig — conferring the full empowerment several times in exile, including to the Rigpa sangha in London (1987) and Prapoutel (1990).20
Late 20th Century
Penor Rinpoche (1932–2009)
3rd Drubwang Pema Norbu, Palyul throneholder, receives the complete Nyingtik Yabzhi transmission with detailed explanations from Dilgo Khyentse, carrying the line into the Palyul network worldwide.21
21st Century
The Nyingtik Yabzhi Translation Project
Launched in September 2023 by Lotsawa House, funded by Tsadra Foundation, advised by Alak Zenkar Rinpoche — a long-term effort to translate the complete 13-volume collection, including the Khandro Nyingthig, into English. The initial focus is the Lama Yangtik, with the Khandro sections to follow.

Khandro Nyingthig and the Wider Nyingthig Stream

It is worth noting that the Khandro Nyingthig’s influence extends far beyond its own textual boundaries. Every subsequent major Nyingthig terma cycle — from Jigme Lingpa’s Longchen Nyingthig, to Dudjom Lingpa’s Dudjom Tersar, to Mingyur Dorje’s Namchö — stands on structural, symbolic, and doctrinal foundations first laid in the Khandro Nyingthig and its companion Vima cycle. When Nyingma practitioners today complete a ngöndro, practice Tröma as yidam, or receive trekchö/tögal instruction, they are drawing on methods whose deepest textual roots reach back through Pema Ledreltsel to the syllable NRI written on Princess Pema Sal’s heart.
