Introduction

✦ The newest current of the Nyingma terma tradition ✦

The Dudjom Tersar — literally the “New Treasures of Dudjom” — is the collective name for the vast body of terma (revealed treasure) teachings brought forth by two remarkable masters of the Nyingma school: the 19th-century visionary Dudjom Lingpa (1835–1903/4) and his recognized reincarnation Dudjom Jigdral Yeshe Dorje (1904–1987), known simply as Dudjom Rinpoche. Together, these two masters revealed one of the most extensive and influential terma cycles of recent centuries — a cycle that has shaped the Nyingma transmission across Tibet, Bhutan, the Himalayas, and the modern diaspora.1

The word tersar (gter gsar) means “new treasure,” distinguishing these recent revelations from the great terma cycles of earlier centuries. But the “newness” is only chronological. By tradition, the content of these teachings was sealed by Guru Padmasambhava in the 8th century and placed in the mindstream of his close disciples, to be discovered at the precise moment when suffering beings would most need them — with the specific Tersar revealer understood to be, in both the case of Dudjom Lingpa and Dudjom Rinpoche, the literal reincarnation of those 8th-century disciples.2

“Through the practice of Tröma Nakmo, thirteen of Dudjom Lingpa’s disciples attained rainbow body, and one thousand reached the level of Vidyādhara.” — Oral tradition of the Dudjom lineage

What follows is an accessible overview of the tradition — the lives of its two central figures, the structure of the revealed cycles, its distinctive practices, and the living holders who continue it into the present day.

What is a Terma?

✦ Hidden teachings revealed in time ✦

To understand the Dudjom Tersar one must first understand what a terma is. In the Nyingma tradition, Guru Padmasambhava — the 8th-century tantric master who established Buddhism in Tibet — is said to have foreseen that his teachings would pass through periods of distortion and decline. Together with his principal consort Yeshe Tsogyal, he is held to have concealed hundreds of teachings for discovery at later, more auspicious times.3

These concealments took two forms. Sater (sa gter, “earth treasures”) are physical objects — texts, statues, or ritual items — hidden in caves, lakes, cliffs, or the walls of temples. Gongter (dgongs gter, “mind treasures”) are teachings transmitted directly into the mindstreams of destined disciples, to surface later as spontaneous revelation. A qualified tertön (treasure-revealer) retrieves and transcribes these teachings under the seal of strict lineage protocols.4

The Dudjom Tersar contains both kinds. The majority of Dudjom Lingpa’s revelations were gongter; Dudjom Rinpoche’s major terma, including the celebrated Pudri Rekpung Vajrakilaya cycle, were also gongter — revealed, in one famous case, during retreat at Paro Taktsang, the tiger’s-nest cave in Bhutan.5

❦ ❦ ❦

Dudjom Lingpa — The Tertön of Golok

✦ Traktung Dudjom Lingpa · 1835–1903/4 ✦

Dudjom Lingpa was born in 1835, in the Wood-Sheep year of the fourteenth sexagenary cycle, in the Golok region of eastern Tibet — a vast, rugged land of nomadic herders beyond the reach of the central Tibetan monastic establishment. Auspicious signs accompanied his birth, and from the age of two or three he is said to have had direct visions of wisdom Ḍākinīs and of Guru Padmasambhava.6

He was formally recognized as an incarnation of the great tertön Rigdzin Düddul Dorje (1615–1672), and through him of Khye’u Chung Lotsawa — one of the twenty-five heart-disciples of Padmasambhava. Unlike many of his contemporaries, Dudjom Lingpa was not primarily a monastic scholar; he was a householder yogi, a ngakpa, and his authority rested on the sheer force of his visionary attainment rather than on institutional rank.7

His biography (namthar) records a continuous stream of visionary encounters — with Padmasambhava and Yeshe Tsogyal, with wisdom Ḍākinīs, with Longchenpa and other masters of the Great Perfection lineage, and with a succession of non-human teachers in pure-vision (dag snang) form. Over his lifetime these revelations accumulated into a massive body of teachings: practice texts, commentaries, entire ritual cycles, and some of the most remarkable philosophical poetry in the Dzogchen canon.8

“Dudjom Lingpa’s literary works, especially those on non-meditation Dzogchen, are highly regarded within the Nyingma school tradition.” — Wikipedia; cross-referenced with Lotsawa House

He passed away in 1903 (some sources give 27 December 1903; Tibetan reckoning sometimes gives 1904), having prophesied that he would be immediately reborn in the hidden land of Pemakö, in southeastern Tibet. That prediction would be fulfilled within the year.9

The Four Major Cycles

✦ Dudjom Lingpa’s revealed treasures ✦

Dudjom Lingpa’s revelations are traditionally organized into four major terma cycles — the first three being gongter (mind treasures) and the last a sater (earth treasure). Each cycle has its own distinctive focus, yet all culminate in the view and practice of Dzogchen, the Great Perfection.10

I · GONGTER

Zabchö Gongpa Rangdrol

The Naturally Liberated Wisdom-Mind

The Maha-Ati Yoga cycle of the “Gathered Peaceful Deities,” containing practices of Chenrezik (Avalokiteśvara) and presentations of the Great Perfection view.

II · GONGTER

Daknang Yeshe Drawa

The Wisdom Net of Pure Visions

Contains chöd (“cutting through”) practices based on the wrathful Ḍākinī Tröma Nakmo (Black Wrathful Mother). A central practice cycle of the tradition.

III · GONGTER

Chönyi Namkhai Longdzö

Vast Space Treasury of the Nature of Reality

A further extensive chöd cycle, again centered on Tröma Nakmo. Regarded as the more detailed companion to the Daknang Yeshe Drawa.

IV · SATER

Zabsang Khandro Nyingtik

Profound Heart-Essence of the Ḍākinīs

The earth-treasure cycle containing the Namchak Pudri Vajrakilaya practice — the “Meteoric-Iron Razor,” later expanded by Dudjom Rinpoche himself.

❦ ❦ ❦

Tröma Nakmo & the Rainbow Body

✦ The Black Wrathful Mother ✦

Tröma Nakmo (khros ma nag mo, “the Black Wrathful One”) is the central wisdom-Ḍākinī of the Dudjom Tersar. She embodies the wrathfully compassionate aspect of primordial wisdom and is the principal deity of the tradition’s chöd practice — the Buddhist ritual of “cutting through” ego-clinging by symbolically offering one’s own body to all beings.11

The practice of Tröma Nakmo chöd is considered among the most profound methods for severing attachment to the self. It is the most beloved and widely performed practice of the Dudjom Tersar, engaged daily by householders, yogis, and monastics alike. The tradition’s oral history records that among Dudjom Lingpa’s direct disciples, thirteen masters attained the rainbow body ('ja' lus) at death — dissolving their physical bodies into pure light — and that a further one thousand reached the level of vidyādhara (knowledge-holder).12

13 disciples attained rainbow body
1,000 reached vidyādhara
4 major terma cycles
25 volumes of collected works

Whatever one makes of such traditional accounts, their persistence within the living tradition — and their corroboration across multiple independent lineage sources — speaks to the extraordinary reputation the Dudjom Tersar earned within a single generation of its revelation.

❦ ❦ ❦

Dudjom Rinpoche — The Continuator

✦ Dudjom Jigdral Yeshe Dorje · 1904–1987 ✦

In keeping with his predecessor’s prophecy, Dudjom Jigdral Yeshe Dorje was born in 1904 in the hidden land of Pemakö — one of the beyul, the “hidden valleys” consecrated by Padmasambhava — in the southeastern reaches of Tibet. From the age of five he himself began to reveal terma, and by his teens he had received the complete transmission of his previous incarnation’s cycles from Dudjom Lingpa’s direct disciples.13

His training was unusually broad. He studied at Mindrolling, Dorje Drak, Tarjé Tingpoling in central Tibet, and at Kathok and Dzogchen in eastern Tibet — receiving empowerments and transmissions from more than fifty teachers across all of the major lineages. This non-sectarian education became the foundation for what is often considered his single greatest scholarly achievement.14

His Own Terma Revelations

Dudjom Rinpoche revealed more than a dozen cycles of his own terma, expanding the Dudjom Tersar into its final, definitive shape. The four most important are:15

Khandro Thugtik

Revealed 1928 · gongter

“The Ḍākinī Heart-Essence” — the principal Ḍākinī practice of the Dudjom Tersar, focused on Yeshe Tsogyal in her outer, inner, secret, and innermost-secret forms.

Pudri Rekpung

Revealed 1937 · gongter

“The Razor that Destroys at a Touch” — Dudjom Rinpoche’s own cycle of Vajrakilaya, revealed while in retreat at Paro Taktsang in Bhutan. A central protective and wrathful practice of the lineage.

Töma Gyurmé

Middle period · gongter

Unchanging Wrathful Mother cycle — another distinctive Tröma-related revelation extending the chöd transmission of Dudjom Lingpa.

Tsasum Pudri

Later revelation

“The Razor of the Three Roots” — a combined guru–deva–Ḍākinī practice that weaves together the three inner Vajrayana objects of refuge.

Head of the Nyingma School

In 1960, after fleeing the Chinese invasion of Tibet, Dudjom Rinpoche was appointed by the 14th Dalai Lama and the Central Tibetan Administration as the first Supreme Head of the Nyingma school in exile — a position he held until his passing at Dordogne, France, in 1987. His efforts to preserve the Nyingma tradition in the diaspora — establishing monasteries, overseeing publications, giving transmissions across Europe, Asia, and North America — are incalculable.16

Among his many scholarly contributions, the single most influential is his two-volume The Nyingma School of Tibetan Buddhism: Its Fundamentals and History. Composed in India in the 1960s and translated into English by Matthew Kapstein and the late Gyurme Dorje (Wisdom Publications, 1991), it remains the single most comprehensive source on the Nyingma tradition in any Western language.17

The Path of Practice

✦ From preliminaries to the Great Perfection ✦

The Three Ngöndros

The Dudjom Tersar is unusual in offering three distinct sets of preliminary practices, each suited to a different level of engagement with the tradition:18

Dudjom Tersar Ngöndro

The brief preliminaries

The most widely practiced — a condensed ngöndro covering refuge, bodhicitta, Vajrasattva purification, maṇḍala offering, and guru yoga, suitable for householder practitioners with limited retreat time.

Khandro Thugtik Ngöndro

Ḍākinī preliminaries

The preliminary phase of the Khandro Thugtik cycle, used by practitioners entering the central Ḍākinī sādhana of the Dudjom Tersar.

Tröma Nakmo Ngöndro

Chöd preliminaries

The preliminaries specific to the Tröma Nakmo chöd cycle, foundational for practitioners who will engage the wrathful-mother practice at depth.

The Main Practices

After the preliminaries, Dudjom Tersar practitioners engage the inner deity practices at the heart of the cycle. Tröma Nakmo chöd is the most widely practiced. Pudri Rekpung Vajrakilaya functions as the primary wrathful practice, used for removing inner and outer obstacles. The Khandro Thugtik cycle provides the Ḍākinī sādhana framework, while peaceful and wrathful guru yogas on Padmasambhava run through the entire tradition.19

The Dzogchen View: “Buddhahood Without Meditation”

The pinnacle of the Dudjom Tersar is its presentation of Dzogchen — and it is here that Dudjom Lingpa’s particular genius shines most clearly. His visionary text Nang-jang, rendered into English as Buddhahood Without Meditation: A Visionary Account Known as Refining One’s Perception, is regarded as one of the most lucid direct-pointing texts ever composed. In a sequence of fourteen visionary encounters with enlightened teachers, it presents the Great Perfection through four themes: nonexistence, oneness, uniform pervasiveness, and spontaneous actualization — the four special samayas of Dzogchen.20

“Buddhahood Without Meditation presents the view of the Great Perfection through the approach known as trekchö — cutting through solidity — developed through the four themes of nonexistence, oneness, uniform pervasiveness, and spontaneous actualization.” — Richard Barron (trans.), Padma Publishing

The full Dzogchen path of the tradition includes both trekchö (cutting through to primordial purity) and tögal (direct crossing to spontaneous presence). Practitioners are traditionally introduced to these teachings only after the preliminaries and main sādhanas have been fulfilled, and always in the direct presence of a qualified master.

The Living Lineage

✦ From Dudjom Rinpoche to the present ✦

On his passing in 1987, Dudjom Rinpoche entrusted the Dudjom Tersar to an extraordinary circle of heart-disciples. Through them — and through their disciples in turn — the tradition continues as a living transmission across the modern world.21

Chatral Sangye Dorje Rinpoche

1913–2015

The foremost Vajra-regent of the Dudjom Tersar in the modern era. A reclusive ngakpa yogi of legendary discipline and vegetarian advocacy (tsethar — the saving of lives). Dudjom Rinpoche personally transmitted to him the complete cycle of the Tersar. He lived to 102.

Dungse Thinley Norbu Rinpoche

1931–2011

The eldest son of Dudjom Rinpoche, a major modern Nyingma teacher and patron of the Vajrayana Foundation. Author of celebrated Dzogchen works including White Sail and Magic Dance.

Yangthang Rinpoche

1930–2016

Domang Yangthang Tulku — a principal holder of the Tersar, recognized as an incarnation of Dorje Dechen Lingpa. Renowned teacher of Tröma Nakmo chöd and the Dzogchen teachings of the cycle.

Lama Tharchin Rinpoche

1936–2013

A student of Chatral Rinpoche and Dudjom Rinpoche, born into the Repkong ngakpa tradition. Founder of the Vajrayana Foundation in the United States and one of the principal transmitters of the Dudjom Tersar in the West.

Dudjom III — Sangye Pema Shepa

1990–2022

The recognized yangsi (reincarnation) of Dudjom Jigdral Yeshe Dorje. Recognized by Chatral Rinpoche, Thinley Norbu Rinpoche, and Minling Trichen Rinpoche. He entered thukdam and passed at his residence in Tibet at the age of 31.

Gyatrul Rinpoche · Orgyen Kusum Lingpa · others

Contemporary holders

Among the many other contemporary transmitters: Kyabjé Gyatrul Rinpoche (1924–2023), Orgyen Kusum Lingpa, Garchen Rinpoche’s students receiving Dudjom transmissions, and the active teaching circle of Dudjom International Foundation in India, Europe, and Hong Kong.

The passing of Dudjom Yangsi Sangye Pema Shepa in 2022 left an unexpected transition in the formal succession of the tradition, but the transmission itself flows through many qualified holders. Today, Dudjom Tersar practice centers operate across Nepal, India, Bhutan, Europe, the United States, Australia, Hong Kong, and beyond — including the Vajrayana Foundation (founded by Lama Tharchin), Yeshe Nyingpo, Orgyen Cho Dzong, Dudjom Buddhist International, and Nyingma Tersar España. The lineage remains remarkably close to its revealed source: most present-day practitioners received empowerment directly from students of Dudjom Rinpoche or Chatral Rinpoche themselves.22

❦ ❦ ❦

A New Current, Ancient Source

✦ Recently revealed, eternally transmitted ✦

The Dudjom Tersar sits at a fascinating place in the Nyingma tradition. Its revelation is recent — less than two centuries old — yet its content claims continuity with Padmasambhava’s 8th-century transmission and through him with the primordial source of all Dzogchen. For its practitioners, the term “new” in “New Treasures” marks not novelty but timeliness: teachings released precisely at the moment when practitioners could most use them.

In the twentieth century, the Dudjom Tersar became one of the vehicles through which Vajrayana Buddhism crossed into the modern world. Its practices of Tröma Nakmo chöd, Pudri Rekpung Vajrakilaya, and Buddhahood Without Meditation’s direct-pointing Dzogchen are now practiced daily in languages the tradition’s founders never heard. Whether one encounters it as a scholar, a practitioner, or an interested observer, the Dudjom Tersar offers a particularly vivid example of Tibetan Buddhism’s capacity for self-renewal — ancient in content, perpetually new in revelation.
