The Terma Tradition
of Padmasambhava

གཏེར་མ  ·  gter ma  ·  "hidden treasure"

A thousand years of revealed scripture, hidden by the Lotus-Born and his Tibetan consort across the eighth-century landscape and unveiled — text by text, vision by vision — across the millennium that followed by more than a thousand prophesied tertöns.

A Deep Research Presentation
Prologue

A Thousand-Year Conversation Across Time

Of all the ways that Tibetan Buddhism has imagined the transmission of sacred teaching across time, none is stranger, more original, or more characteristic of the Nyingma school than the terma tradition. The Tibetan word gter ma means simply "treasure" — but what is being treasured, and how, is unlike anything in the ordinary religious history of the world. A terma is a teaching deliberately concealed, in the eighth century, by Padmasambhava and Yeshe Tsogyal, in physical objects, in landscapes, or in the awareness-streams of disciples — sealed for a future moment when the world would need it, and intended to surface, in that future moment, through a particular prophesied person: a tertön, a "treasure revealer."1

The first tertön, by traditional account, was Sangye Lama (1000–1080).19 Since him there have been more than a thousand. They have produced a body of sacred Tibetan literature so vast that the Rinchen Terdzö — the great nineteenth-century compendium of terma, compiled by Jamgön Kongtrul Lodrö Tayé — runs to sixty-three volumes, and the wider extant terma corpus runs to many times that. Every one of those texts carries the same claim: I was hidden in the eighth century by Padmasambhava himself; I am surfacing now because the time has come.2

Definition

What Terma Actually Is

To understand terma, one must first set aside the ordinary Western category of "discovered ancient text." A terma is not, in the tradition's own self-understanding, a manuscript that someone happened to find in a cave. It is a teaching that was placed there — by Padmasambhava — for this tertön, in this historical moment, with full prophetic knowledge of when, by whom, and for whose benefit it would surface. The placing and the revealing are two halves of one act, separated by a thousand years.3

The mechanism by which Padmasambhava is said to have accomplished this involves the technique of mind-mandate transmission (gtad rgya): an empowerment in which a teaching is sealed not in ink but in the awareness-continuum of a disciple, with a prophecy of the future life — sometimes hundreds of years later — in which that disciple will reincarnate, recover the teaching, and bring it forth.4

Through this technique, Yeshe Tsogyal — Padmasambhava's chief Tibetan consort, said in the tradition to possess perfect recall — wrote down his oral teachings in dakini script, a coded symbolic language understood only by the awakened. Padmasambhava and his disciples then concealed these scripts and their associated objects (statues, relics, ritual implements) in physical hiding places throughout Tibet, Bhutan, and the Himalayan borderlands, while parallel teachings were sealed directly into the mindstreams of the future tertöns themselves.5

"Padmasambhava's oral teachings to his chief disciple Yeshe Tsogyal, Princess of Kharchen, were recorded in coded language called dakini script and concealed for centuries — to be revealed by tertöns many centuries later." — Erik Pema Kunsang, introduction to Dakini Teachings

The Short Lineage

One of the unusual structural features of terma in the Nyingma tradition is that it is a "near" or "short" lineage. Where ordinary kama transmission descends through dozens of human teachers across centuries — with all the attendant risks of dilution, loss, or corruption — a terma reaches its tertön directly from Padmasambhava, with only one human link in between. That link is Padmasambhava's prophesied disciple, who reincarnated in the present age as the tertön. The transmission is therefore considered uniquely fresh, uniquely potent, and uniquely matched to the conditions of the time of its emergence.6

Typology

The Three Modes of Treasure

The tradition classifies terma into three principal modes, distinguished by where the treasure is hidden and how it is recovered:

Sa-ter

Earth Treasure

Physically concealed in the landscape — caves, rocks, lakes, statues, pillars, temples. The tertön is led to the location by prophecies, dreams, or visions. There he or she retrieves a yellow scroll inscribed in dakini script, and the act of seeing the script triggers full memory of the teaching, which the tertön then transcribes into Tibetan.

Gong-ter

Mind Treasure

Concealed not in the world but in the awareness-stream of the disciple-tertön. No physical object is involved. The teaching surfaces from within — in deep meditation, in lucid dreams, or in symbolic visions — at the moment Padmasambhava had prophesied. Often the tertön writes spontaneously, scarcely able to keep up with what is arising.

Dak-nang

Pure Vision

A revelation arising from the realized master's direct visionary encounter with awakened beings — Padmasambhava, Yeshe Tsogyal, deities, dakinis. Distinct from gong-ter in that dak-nang does not necessarily claim ancient origin; it is fresh wisdom, granted in the present, on the strength of the master's realization.

The three modes are not always cleanly separable in practice. A given tertön's corpus may contain works of all three types, and individual revelations may have features of more than one. Dudjom Lingpa (1835–1904), for example, produced three gong-ter cycles and one sa-ter cycle — twenty volumes in all — across his life, with significant dak-nang elements interlaced throughout.7

Eighth Century

Padmasambhava and Yeshe Tsogyal — The Concealers

Padmasambhava — the Lotus-Born, Guru Rinpoche — arrived in Tibet around 770 at the invitation of King Trisong Detsen and the abbot Śāntarakṣita to subdue the obstacles preventing the establishment of Buddhism. His traditional biography is itself a treasure-revealed text: the Pemathangyik (Padma bka' thang) was revealed centuries later by Orgyen Lingpa (1323–c.1360) and remains the principal source for his life-story. After taming the country's protective spirits and consecrating Samye Monastery, he turned his attention to the vast project that would shape Tibetan Buddhism's deepest temporal logic: the deliberate concealment of teachings for the future.8

His chief Tibetan partner in this enterprise was Yeshe Tsogyal (c. 757–817), Princess of Kharchen, who is said in the tradition to have possessed total recall — every word she heard, she could repeat verbatim, sometimes years later, sometimes through the mediation of awakened recollection. Padmasambhava taught; she listened; she encoded what she had heard in dakini script, and together they concealed manuscripts, statues, ritual implements, and entire mandalas of practice in lakes, mountainsides, pillar-cavities, and remote cliffs across the Tibetan plateau and the Himalayan borderlands.9

The reason most often given in the traditional sources is twofold. First: certain teachings were too potent — too easily distorted, too liable to harm in the wrong hands — to circulate openly in the eighth century. Second: Padmasambhava foresaw that the tradition itself would face a crisis. King Langdarma's persecution (838–841) would nearly end Tibetan Buddhism within a generation; later, internal sectarian quarrels, foreign invasions, and the calamities of the twentieth century would each take their toll. The concealed treasures were a survival mechanism — and more than a survival mechanism: they were a way of making the teaching responsive to time, releasing each cycle precisely when it would do most good.10

"He concealed these as treasures in various places — including rocks, lakes, temples, statues, and in the mindstream of the recipient — and prophesied that in the future, these disciples would reincarnate, reveal these teachings from their place of concealment, and spread them for the sake of beings." — Treasury of Lives, The Modes of Transmission of Nyingma Teachings
The Future Vessels

The Twenty-Five — Lord and Subjects

The pivotal figures in this whole architecture are the "Lord and Subjects" — the twenty-five (rje 'bangs nyer lnga): Padmasambhava's twenty-five principal Tibetan disciples, who included King Trisong Detsen himself, Yeshe Tsogyal, the great translator Vairotsana, Namkhai Nyingpo, Nyak Jñānakumāra, and others. To each of these twenty-five Padmasambhava transmitted specific teachings, sealed those teachings into their awareness-streams through mind-mandate transmission, and prophesied the future life in which each disciple would reincarnate as a tertön and reveal the teaching he had been entrusted with.11

Every authentic tertön of the subsequent millennium is therefore traditionally regarded as the rebirth of one or another of these twenty-five — sometimes named explicitly in the prophecies, sometimes identified retrospectively by recognized masters. Pema Lingpa is said to be a rebirth of Princess Pema Sal; Jigme Lingpa of King Trisong Detsen and (in another aspect) of Vimalamitra; Chokgyur Lingpa of Murub Tsenpo (a son of Trisong Detsen); and so on. The system is not arbitrary: it specifies which tertöns can reveal which teachings, and the appearance of a particular treasure becomes a criterion for the recognition of a particular incarnate.12

The Sovereign Five

The Five Tertön Kings

Among the thousand-plus historical tertöns, the tradition singles out five as the Five Sovereign Tertöns or Five Tertön Kings — gter ston rgyal po lnga. These five are not necessarily the most prolific or the most chronologically continuous; they are the five whose treasure-cycles came to occupy the most central place in the practice life of the Nyingma tradition.13

1124–1192
Nyangrel Nyima Özer
The first of the Five Tertön Kings, recognized as a rebirth of King Trisong Detsen. His most influential revelation is the Kagyé Deshek Düpa ("Embodiment of the Sugatas of the Eight Great Sadhana Teachings"), still practiced today; he also revealed the foundational biography of Padmasambhava, the Zanglingma.
1212–1270
Guru Chöwang (Guru Chökyi Wangchuk)
Second of the Five. Among his most influential revelations: the Lama Sangdü ("Secret Embodiment of the Guru"), which gathers the inner-sadhana practices of the Eight Great Mandalas, and a wealth of teachings on the Eight Heruka Mandalas.
1346–1405
Dorje Lingpa
Third of the Five. Significant for revealing both Buddhist and Bön terma, an unusual cross-traditional achievement — and for opening hidden lands (beyul) in eastern Bhutan and southern Tibet that have remained sacred geography for centuries.
1450–1521
Pema Lingpa
Fourth and, by Bhutanese reckoning, foremost of the Five — second only in importance to Padmasambhava in Bhutan's religious self-understanding. Famous for his retrieval of terma from the depths of Burning Lake (Mebar Tsho) in the Tang valley with a butter lamp burning underwater, witnessed by crowds. His treasures form the basis of major Bhutanese liturgies still performed today.20
1820–1892
Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo
Fifth and last of the Five Tertön Kings. Co-founder of the nineteenth-century Rimé (non-sectarian) movement; recognized as the rebirth of Chetsün Sengé Wangchuk; revealer of the Chetsün Nyingthig and dozens of other cycles. Together with Jamgön Kongtrul, he gathered, edited, and re-transmitted the entire Nyingma terma heritage at a moment when it might otherwise have begun to dissipate.
The Eight Great Lingpas

The Eight Great Lingpas & Other Major Tertöns

Beyond the Five Sovereign Tertöns, the tradition recognizes a second cardinal grouping: the Eight Great Lingpas (gling pa brgyad) — eight tertöns whose names share the suffix -lingpa ("isle" or "continent"). Their cycles, alongside the Five Kings', shape the mainstream of Nyingma practice.14

1295–1375

Rinchen Lingpa

The first of the eight, an early Bhutanese tertön of the fourteenth century.

1340–1396

Sangye Lingpa

Revealer of the Lama Gongdü ("Embodiment of the Realization of the Guru") in thirteen volumes — one of the most extensive single terma cycles ever revealed.

1346–1405

Dorje Lingpa

Also counted among the Five Kings; cross-tradition revealer of Buddhist and Bön cycles.

14th c.

Karma Lingpa

Revealer of the Shyitro Gongpa Rangdrol — the cycle of "Self-Liberation through the Intent of the Peaceful and Wrathful Deities." From this cycle comes the Bardo Tödrol Chenmo, known in the West as the Tibetan Book of the Dead.21

1403–1471

Ratna Lingpa

Compiler of the Nyingma Gyübum — the first systematic collection of the Nyingma tantras — alongside his own treasure-revelations.

1408–1489

Kunkyong Lingpa

Major fifteenth-century tertön with cycles preserved within the Rinchen Terdzö.

1450–1521

Pema Lingpa

Of the Five Kings; the dominant Bhutanese tertön (his Burning Lake recovery is the iconic image of the terma tradition).

1480–1535

Padma Tsewang Gyalpo

The eighth Lingpa in the standard enumeration; a 16th-century tertön of significant influence in eastern Tibet.

Other Cardinal Tertöns

Beyond the formal groupings, several other tertöns are pivotal to the mainstream of Nyingma practice and deserve mention here:

Sangye Lama (1000–1080)

The first tertön. Begins the entire historical tradition of treasure-revelation, four centuries after Padmasambhava's concealments.

Pema Ledreltsel (1291–1315)

Brief-lived but consequential: revealer of the Khandro Nyingthig, prefiguring his own next rebirth as Longchenpa.

Orgyen Lingpa (1323–c.1360)

Revealer of the Pemathangyik, the standard biography of Padmasambhava himself — itself a terma.

Jigme Lingpa (1730–1798)

Revealer of the Longchen Nyingthig — a gong-ter arising from three visions of Longchenpa, and today the most widely practiced single Dzogchen cycle in the Nyingma world.

Chokgyur Dechen Lingpa (1829–1870)

Friend, collaborator, and visionary partner of Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo and Jamgön Kongtrul. His many cycles, especially the Lamey Tukdrub Barchey Künsel, are central to nineteenth-century and modern Kagyü-Nyingma joint practice.

Dudjom Lingpa & Dudjom Rinpoche

The 19th–20th-century father–emanation pair whose Dudjom Tersar ("New Treasures of Dudjom") forms one of the most actively practiced contemporary terma cycles, especially in the Nyingma diaspora.

Discernment

Authentication, Controversy & the Question of Truth

Because terma claims a very specific kind of authority — direct from Padmasambhava, untouched by intervening centuries — it has always been accompanied by an equally specific need for authentication. The tradition has elaborated criteria over centuries: prophecies in the Padmasambhava biographies that name the future tertön; the presence of a "treasure custodian" (chödak) appointed by previous prophecy; the recognition by senior masters of the tertön's identity as a particular reborn disciple; signs at the time of revelation (rainbows, trembling earth, dakini song, the witness of the local people); and the internal coherence and yogic efficacy of the revealed cycle when subsequently practiced.15

These standards have not prevented the appearance, across the centuries, of terma claims that the wider Nyingma community subsequently rejected as inauthentic. The tradition has its own internal critical apparatus — and it has used it. Senior masters in each generation have written authoritative judgments of which recent terma are reliable and which are not.

The Sakya Pandita Critique

The most famous external critique came from outside the Nyingma school: from Sakya Pandita Kunga Gyaltsen (1182–1251), the great Sakya scholar, whose Distinguishing the Three Vows argued that "fabricated tantras" had crept into Tibet under the cover of terma-status and that vigilance against apocrypha was a religious duty. Sakya Pandita's was a measured argument, not a rejection of terma as such — and indeed, by the 14th century the Sakya school itself was receiving terma teachings beginning with their third throne-holder. But his critique remains the classic statement of the skeptical case, and the tradition's serious answer to it produced a more rigorous internal authentication standard than would otherwise have emerged.16

Modern Scholarship

Contemporary academic study of terma begins, in English, with Janet Gyatso's landmark Apparitions of the Self: The Secret Autobiographies of a Tibetan Visionary (Princeton, 1998), a translation and study of two extraordinary self-narratives by Jigme Lingpa. Gyatso's work treats terma as a distinctive Tibetan literary genre with its own conventions, semiotics, and metaphysics — and provides what remains the clearest scholarly account of the metaphysical process by which a treasure-text is held to be transmitted, the role of the dakini in that transmission, and the apparent paradox of an "advanced Buddhist practitioner steeped in 'no-self' producing an autobiography about his spiritual achievements."17

Other significant scholarly contributions include Tulku Thondup's Hidden Teachings of Tibet (Wisdom, 1986), which presents the tradition from within; David Germano and Janet Gyatso's "Longchenpa and the Possession of the Dakinis" (in Tantra in Practice, 2000); and the ongoing translation and analysis work of the Padmakara, Lotsawa House, and Tsadra Foundation projects, which have made significant portions of the Five Kings' and Eight Lingpas' cycles available in English for the first time.18

Into the Present

The Living Tradition

What is striking about terma as a tradition is that it has not stopped. It is not, like the canon-formation of most religious traditions, a closed event in deep history. New terma have continued to appear in every generation — including the twentieth and twenty-first — and there is no traditional reason to expect them to stop.

The principal twentieth-century examples illustrate the continuing vitality of the form. Dudjom Lingpa (1835–1904), and his recognized emanation Dudjom Rinpoche (1904–1987), produced the Dudjom Tersar — twenty volumes of treasures, the last cycle of which was a sa-ter revealed under conditions of remarkable witness. Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche (1910–1991) revealed his own cycles within the Khyentse line. Chatral Rinpoche (1913–2015), one of the most respected Nyingma yogins of the modern era, was an authenticated tertön. The pattern continues into the twenty-first century with masters such as Khandro Täre Lhamo (1938–2002) and Namkhai Norbu (1938–2018).

The Great Compendia

Two great nineteenth-century compendia preserve the bulk of the tradition's textual heritage:

Rinchen Terdzö (Treasury of Precious Termas)

Compiled by Jamgön Kongtrul Lodrö Tayé (1813–1899) under the encouragement of Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo. Sixty-three volumes gathering the most important terma from the eleventh through the nineteenth centuries — the indispensable single reference for any practicing master of the broader Nyingma terma heritage.22

Damngak Dzö & the Five Treasuries

Kongtrul's wider Five Treasuries project, of which the Rinchen Terdzö is one, also includes the Damngak Dzö (Treasury of Pith Instructions), the Kagyü Ngakdzö, the Sheja Dzö, and the Gyachen Kadzö — collectively a Rimé ("non-sectarian") preservation effort that saved tens of thousands of pages of practice-literature from the brink of loss.

The institutional inheritance of the terma tradition today flows through the Nyingma diaspora — Mindrolling, Palyul, Dzogchen, Shechen, and Kathok monasteries and their associated centers worldwide — and through the lay practice traditions of Bhutan and the Himalayan borderlands. Translation projects (notably Lotsawa House, the Padmakara Translation Group, and the Tsadra Foundation initiatives) are now, for the first time, making the bulk of the tradition's textual base accessible in English and other Western languages.

"Since the first tertön Sangye Lama, right up until the present day, there have been over a thousand tertöns — and the tradition continues. New terma may appear in any generation in which the conditions and the prophesied person come together." — Treasury of Lives, on the modes of Nyingma transmission
