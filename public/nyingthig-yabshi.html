Nyingthig Yabshi
The Fourfold Heart Essence

སྙིང་ཐིག་ཡ་བཞི  ·  snying thig ya bzhi

The thirteen-volume compendium in which Longchen Rabjam wove together the two great streams of Dzogchen innermost-essence transmission — Vimalamitra's line from India and Padmasambhava's line through the dakinis — into a single, complete corpus that has anchored the Nyingma menngagde transmission ever since.

A Deep Research Presentation
Prologue

The Treasury That Contains All the Other Treasuries

Among the vast literatures of Tibetan Buddhism, one collection holds a place apart: the Nyingthig Yabshi, or Fourfold Heart Essence. It is not a single revelation; it is not a single author's work. It is the great compendium — the collection in which the fourteenth-century master Longchen Rabjam (Longchenpa, 1308–1364) gathered, edited, and synthesized the whole extant body of the oldest Dzogchen pith-instruction (menngagde) teachings into one coherent, thirteen-volume architecture.1

In doing so, Longchenpa solved a problem the Nyingma tradition had carried for five centuries. By his time the Dzogchen "Heart-Essence" instruction — the innermost level of the Great Perfection — had reached Tibet along two parallel lines: the Vima Nyingthig from the Indian paṇḍita Vimalamitra, transmitted to Tibet in the 780s under King Trisong Detsen, and the Khandro Nyingthig from Padmasambhava, sealed in dakini form and hidden as terma. Each line had its own history of concealment, rainbow-body transmission, rediscovery, and near-loss. Each had its own commentaries and practice cycles. Each was nearly impossible to practice by itself.2

Longchenpa united them. He re-edited both root cycles, composed a companion commentary to each — the Lama Yangtig for the Vima, the Khandro Yangtig for the Khandro — and then wrote a third, synthesizing work, the Zabmo Yangtig, that distills the essence of both. The resulting five-part, thirteen-volume collection is the Nyingthig Yabshi.3

"The Nyingthig Yabshi is the practice aspect of Longchenpa's writings and the basis of the Old Nyingthig — a thirteen-volume collection in which he synthesized the Vima Nyingtik of Vimalamitra and the Khandro Nyingtik of Guru Rinpoche, and explained all the practical details in the light of his own realization." — Rigpa Wiki, Nyingtik Yabshyi

Philology

What "Nyingthig Yabshi" Means

The Tibetan snying thig ya bzhi translates word by word as "heart-drop, the fourfold." Nyingthig (snying thig) — "heart essence" or "heart drop" — is the technical name for the innermost, most distilled class of Dzogchen teachings: the pith-instruction (menngagde) cycle, considered the pinnacle of the three classes of Dzogchen (semde, longde, menngagde).4 Yabshi (ya bzhi) means "having four parts" or "the fourfold set." Thus: The Fourfold Heart Essence.5

The "four" in the title is not arbitrary. It reflects a traditional architecture of two mothers (ma) and two children (bu): two root-tantra cycles and their two companion commentaries. Longchenpa's fifth work, the Zabmo Yangtig, is the synthesizing key that ties the four together — sometimes counted separately, sometimes as the crown of the fourfold architecture.5

Origins

Two Streams, One Ocean

The Nyingthig Yabshi carries two distinct streams of transmission. Both descend, in the traditional Nyingma account, from the primordial Buddha Samantabhadra through the dharmakāya lineage, into the sambhogakāya vidyādhara Vajrasattva, and then into the human world through Garab Dorje, the first human Dzogchen lineage holder. From Garab Dorje the teachings flow to Mañjuśrīmitra, then to Śrī Siṃha — and from Śrī Siṃha the two streams diverge.6

The Indian Stream · Vima Nyingthig

Śrī Siṃha — Jñānasūtra — Vimalamitra — into Tibet (late 8th c.) — Nyang Tingdzin Zangpo and four others. Concealed at Samye Chimphu. Later restored through Chetsün Sengé Wangchuk's rainbow-body transmission and through direct terma rediscovery.

The Dakini Stream · Khandro Nyingthig

Śrī Siṃha — Padmasambhava — Princess Pema Sal (sealed by the syllable NRI) and Yeshe Tsogyal — concealed in the Dakpo region. Recovered as terma by Pema Ledreltsel (r. 1313) and transmitted through Gyalse Lekden to Longchenpa.

By the fourteenth century, both streams had reached Longchenpa: the Vima through the Chetsün line of oral transmission, the Khandro through the short, recently revealed terma line. He received them both — and recognized that together they formed a single complete system.

Late 8th Century

Vimalamitra — The Master Who Comes Every Hundred Years

Vimalamitra (dri med bshes gnyen, "Stainless Friend") was, by traditional account, born in Western India. He trained under three of the greatest Indian Dzogchen masters — Buddhaguhya, Jñānasūtra, and Śrī Siṃha — and received from them the complete transmission of the Great Perfection. He is regarded, alongside Padmasambhava and Vairotsana, as one of the three founding forefathers of Dzogchen in Tibet, and in particular as the foremost transmitter of the Instruction Class (menngagde).7

Invited by emissaries of King Trisong Detsen, Vimalamitra came to Tibet in the late 8th century. Over thirteen years at the royal court and at Samye monastery, he translated tantras, established the Dzogchen menngagde transmission, and, in the room known as Atsé Barkhang at Samye, bestowed the Innermost Secret Nyingthig of Dzogpachenpo in strictest secrecy to five disciples: King Trisong Detsen himself, Prince Muné Tsenpo, Nyangben Tingdzin Zangpo, and the great translators Kawa Paltsek and Chokro Lui Gyaltsen.8

Before leaving Tibet, Vimalamitra concealed the texts of his innermost teaching at Samye Chimphu, sealing them for the sake of future generations — a precaution, it turned out, that saved the lineage during the dark age of persecution under King Langdarma. Vimalamitra himself departed for Wutai Shan, the sacred five-peaked mountain in China, where tradition holds he remains to this day in a rainbow body, promising to send an emanation back to Tibet every hundred years so long as the teaching endures.9

"The quintessence of Vimalamitra's teaching is known as the Vima Nyingthig — one of the Heart-Essence teachings of the Great Perfection. Having translated these extremely profound instructions, he concealed the texts at Samye Chimphu for the sake of future generations." — Treasury of Lives, Vimalamitra
9th–13th Centuries

The Hidden Centuries — Rainbow Body in the Dark Age

Of Vimalamitra's five Tibetan disciples, Nyang Tingdzin Zangpo became the principal preserver. He practiced the Vima Nyingthig for fifty-five years, attaining ultimate realization, and then — knowing that a time of persecution was coming — hid the explanatory tantras in various locations within the Uru Shai Lhakhang temple, so that the teaching would survive whatever came.10

When the persecution came (under King Langdarma, r. 838–841), the Nyingma kama-lineage (oral) tantras of Dzogchen were indeed nearly lost. But Nyang Tingdzin Zangpo's hidden texts remained. He transmitted the oral pith-instructions to Bé Lodrö Wangchuk, attained the rainbow body — his physical form dissolving into light — and passed from the world.10

The Chetsün Line

The oral-instruction stream then passed from Bé Lodrö Wangchuk to Dangma Lhundrup Gyaltsen, and from him to the eleventh-century master Chetsün Sengé Wangchuk. Chetsün Sengé Wangchuk's role was pivotal: for an entire month he received a direct visionary transmission from Vimalamitra himself, who appeared to him in his wisdom body and bestowed the complete Vima Nyingthig.11

At the age of 125, having completed his mission of preserving and transmitting the cycle, Chetsün Sengé Wangchuk attained the rainbow body — vanishing bodily into the sky in a cloud of rainbow light. The Vima Nyingthig thus reached the fourteenth century partly as "hidden treasure" (terma) and partly as "unbroken oral transmission" (kama) — a dual tradition of exceptional purity precisely because it had been carried, at key junctures, by masters who had literally left the ordinary world in bodies of light.11

The Dakini Line

Meanwhile, the Khandro Nyingthig had lain completely concealed. Padmasambhava's transmission to Princess Pema Sal was sealed by the wrathful dakini-protectress Shaza Khamoche at the rock of Daklha Tramo Drak in Dakpo, and would remain hidden until the early fourteenth century — when Pema Sal's rebirth, the tertön Pema Ledreltsel, revealed the cycle in 1313, transmitted it to his chief disciple Gyalse Lekden, and — dying young — predicted his own next rebirth, who would complete the work: Longchen Rabjam.12

1308–1364

Longchen Rabjam — The Compiler

Longchenpa is, by broad consensus, the greatest scholar-yogin in the entire Nyingma tradition — an astonishing polymath whose collected writings run to more than 250 titles spanning sutra, tantra, philosophy, poetry, and pith-instruction. Born in 1308 in central Tibet, identified as the reincarnation of Pema Ledreltsel, he received both Nyingthig streams during his early years and devoted the rest of his life to preserving, editing, and transmitting them.13

From Kumārāja, his root guru, Longchenpa received the full Vima Nyingthig line descended through Chetsün Sengé Wangchuk. From Gyalse Lekden, he received the Khandro Nyingthig in its newly revealed form. Then — at his hermitage at Gangri Thökar, south of Lhasa — he began the work of his maturity: composing his own companion treasures and commentaries, editing the mother cycles for clarity, and constructing the Nyingthig Yabshi as a single architectural whole.13

What Longchenpa produced was not a simple anthology. It was a re-organization of an entire literature in the light of his own realization. In his editing and his commentaries, he reconciled apparent differences between the two streams; he filled in missing empowerment liturgies; he wrote new pith-instruction manuals where the root texts had only offered aphorisms; and he composed synthesizing works that drew the whole together. The Nyingthig Yabshi is — in the phrase most often used by later tradition — "the practice-aspect of Longchenpa's writings."14

Structure

The Fourfold Architecture

The logic of the Nyingthig Yabshi is a pairing of mothers and children. Each of the two root-terma cycles (the "mothers," ma yig) is accompanied by Longchenpa's own companion cycle (the "children," bu yig) — composed partly as commentary, partly as revealed mind-treasure (dgongs gter). A fifth, synthesizing collection, the Zabmo Yangtig, draws the essence of both lines into one integrated path.15

Mother · Indian Line
Vima Nyingthig
The Heart Essence of Vimalamitra — root terma & kama texts preserving the late 8th-century Indian transmission.
Child · Longchenpa's Commentary
Lama Yangtik
The Wish-Fulfilling Jewel — 35 treatises expanding the Vima cycle, with its four empowerments (elaborate, simple, extremely simple, utterly simple).
Mother · Dakini Line
Khandro Nyingthig
The Heart Essence of the Dakinis — Padmasambhava's transmission to Princess Pema Sal, revealed in 1313 by Pema Ledreltsel.
Child · Longchenpa's Commentary
Khandro Yangtik
The Innermost Essence of the Dakinis — Longchenpa's mind-treasure companion, written amid visions of Guru Rinpoche and Yeshe Tsogyal.
Synthesis · The Key
Zabmo Yangtik
The Profound Quintessence — Longchenpa's own distillation drawing both mothers and both children into a single, compressed path of practice. Often regarded as the most concentrated distillation of the entire collection.

This fourfold (or strictly speaking fivefold) architecture is the reason the collection is called the Yabshi — "Fourfold" — even though the thirteen-volume edition contains five component cycles. The "four" refers to the mother/child pairing: two mothers, two children. The Zabmo Yangtig stands above, or outside, the enumeration: it is the synthesizing crown of the whole architecture.15

"The Vima Nyingtik and Khandro Nyingtik are the 'mother' Nyingtik texts; the Lama Yangtik and Khandro Yangtik are the 'child' texts — hence another common name for the collection, the Four Mother and Child Sections of Nyingtik." — Rangjung Yeshe Wiki, Nyingtik Yabshi
Contents

The Thirteen Volumes

The most widely known edition of the Nyingthig Yabshi runs to thirteen volumes. The ordering below follows the traditional Adzom Chögar and Derge arrangements used in most modern printings.16

Volumes 1–2

Lama Yangtik

Longchenpa's Wish-Fulfilling Jewel — his 35-treatise commentary cycle on the Vima Nyingthig, mainly grounded in the Dzogchen Garland of Pearls tantra. Contains the four empowerments and their pith instructions.

Volumes 3–6

Vima Nyingthig

The root "mother" cycle transmitted from Vimalamitra — four volumes encompassing tantra, sadhana, empowerment liturgy, and pith instruction, including the core Seventeen Tantras reference framework.

Volumes 7–9

Khandro Yangtik

Longchenpa's three-volume Innermost Essence of the Dakinis — his mind-treasure companion to the Khandro Nyingthig. Central works include the Precious Treasury of Meaningful Words, the Precious Golden Garland, and the Cloud Oceans of the Profound Meaning.

Volumes 10–11

Khandro Nyingthig

The root "mother" cycle of Padmasambhava's line: the Six Tantras of Liberation-by-Wearing and the Three Testaments of the Buddha, with their associated empowerments and instruction texts, as revealed by Pema Ledreltsel.

Volumes 12–13

Zabmo Yangtik

Longchenpa's two-volume Profound Quintessence — the synthesizing cycle drawing together the key-points of both mother streams into a single compressed path, itself subsequently used by Jigme Lingpa as a structural model for the Longchen Nyingthig.

All Volumes

Scope of the Whole

More than 380 individual texts; genres ranging from history, biography, and lineage-narrative to tantra, sādhana, empowerment ritual, offering liturgy, dakini-song, and pith-instruction manuals. Arguably the most comprehensive single corpus of Dzogchen menngagde preserved in any language.

The 13-volume Adzom Chögar edition was published at Derge in 1808, combining the Lama Yangtik and Khandro Yangtik collections into print form alongside the root cycles; contemporary scholarly editions (including those catalogued at Rangjung Yeshe Texts Archive and at the Buddhist Digital Resource Center) follow substantially the same structure.17

Into the Present

The Living Lineage

Because Longchenpa compiled the Yabshi to serve as the complete menngagde curriculum of the Nyingma tradition, the collection has been a living, actively transmitted body of teaching for nearly seven centuries. Every major Dzogchen master since Longchenpa has held at least some portion of it, and many have held the whole.

14th Century
Longchen Rabjam (1308–1364)
Compiles the Yabshi at Gangri Thökar; composes the Lama Yangtik, Khandro Yangtik, and Zabmo Yangtig; transmits the complete Khandro Nyingthig to eight fortunate disciples at Samye Chimphu.
18th Century
Jigme Lingpa (1730–1798)
Receives the entire Yabshi transmission; in a further series of three visions, Longchenpa himself appears and compresses the lineage into a "short" transmission. Jigme Lingpa then reveals the Longchen Nyingthig — a gongter cycle that functions as a condensed, practice-oriented refraction of the Yabshi's essentials. Between Longchenpa and Jigme Lingpa there are otherwise fourteen intervening masters.18
19th Century
Patrul, Nyoshul, Khenpo Ngaga
The line passes from Jigme Lingpa to Jigme Gyalwe Nyugu, then to Patrul Rinpoche, then to Nyoshul Lungtok Tenpe Nyima, who transmits to Khenchen Ngakgi Wangpo (Khenpo Ngaga). Each was a principal pillar of the Yabshi transmission in his generation.19
19th Century · Rimé
Jamyang Khyentse Wangpo (1820–1892)
The co-founder of the Rimé (non-sectarian) movement, recognized as the rebirth of Chetsün Sengé Wangchuk himself. Preserves and re-transmits the full Yabshi, and reveals the Chetsün Nyingtik as a gongter drawing directly on Vimalamitra's teachings — a fresh revelation from within the same lineage he had held eight hundred years earlier.
20th Century
Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche (1910–1991)
The principal holder of the Khyentse lineage after the passing of Jamyang Khyentse Chökyi Lodrö. By the age of sixty-four, he had given the combined empowerments of the Nyingthig Yabshi and the Longchen Nyingthig more than ten times; the Jigme Lingpa oral transmission, more rarely — twice in Bhutan and once in India. Carried the complete transmission into the Nyingma diaspora.20
Late 20th Century
Penor Rinpoche (1932–2009)
3rd Drubwang Pema Norbu, Palyul throneholder, receives the complete Nyingthig Yabshi with detailed textual explanation from Dilgo Khyentse — carrying the line into the Palyul monastic network worldwide and ensuring that formal study of the Yabshi continued in exile.
2023–
Nyingtik Yabzhi Translation Project
Launched September 2023 under Lotsawa House with funding from the Tsadra Foundation, senior advisor Alak Zenkar Rinpoche. The first large-scale scholarly effort to translate the complete thirteen-volume collection into English. Initial focus: the Lama Yangtig section, with the Vima Nyingthig and Khandro cycles to follow.21

The Yabshi's Place in Today's Nyingma World

Every living Nyingma Dzogchen lineage draws, at some level, on the Nyingthig Yabshi. Jigme Lingpa's Longchen Nyingthig is structurally a Yabshi-derivative; Dudjom Lingpa and Dudjom Rinpoche's Tersar cycles refine Yabshi practice methods with new terma; the Namchö of Mingyur Dorje is a parallel stream that nevertheless operates on the same underlying menngagde framework. When contemporary Dzogchen teachers speak of trekchö and tögal, of the four visions, of rainbow body, of pointing-out transmission and the four empowerments — they are speaking from a vocabulary that Longchenpa, working with what Vimalamitra and Padmasambhava had left behind, assembled and stabilized into a single coherent architecture seven hundred years ago.22
